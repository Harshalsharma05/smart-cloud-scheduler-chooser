\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Electric grids increasingly ask large consumers to cut their load for a few hours when demand peaks~\cite{liu13}. GPU clusters for machine learning can be such loads, and a cluster that sheds load on request must know what each way of shedding costs.

A GPU cluster has three ways to cut power during an event. It can delay jobs, cap the power of its GPUs, or burst running jobs to a public cloud. Data-center demand response has been studied, including a field test on a production AI cluster~\cite{liu13,zhou24,zhou24flex,colangelo26}, but offloading to the cloud as a priced response to a grid request is understudied. Its key difficulty is that the cloud bill covers a burst job's whole remaining runtime, while the grid values only the hours of the event. Bursting therefore pays off only for jobs that will end soon and succeed, so the cluster needs predictions of the remaining runtime and the failure risk of the jobs running at the event.

We study this choice with trace-driven simulations of two production GPU clusters: the 2017 Microsoft Philly deep-learning cluster~\cite{jeon19} and Seren, a 2023 cluster for developing large language models from AcmeTrace~\cite{hu24}. Grid events last 1 to 4 hours and ask for cuts of 10 to 30\%. We price every action in dollars per MWh shed, and we build a controller that mixes the three actions and chooses jobs using predictions from learned models: a small transformer over each user's job history, and gradient-boosted trees. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A cost model that prices delay, power capping and cloud bursting in dollars per MWh shed on one scale (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).
\item A mix controller that plans over cap levels and job actions, and a predictor interface that any runtime and failure model can fill (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
\item Findings on two traces (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). Delay is the cheapest action that meets the cut on both. For a 2-hour, 20\% cut it costs \$\DefDelayUsdZero{}--\$\DefDelayUsdOne{} per MWh on Philly and \$\AcmeDefDelayUsdZero{}--\$\AcmeDefDelayUsdOne{} on Acme ($w_f=0$ and~1), while bursting alone costs a median of \BurstOverDelayDefaultZero{} (Philly) and \AcmeBurstOverDelayDefaultZero{} (Acme) times as much as delay per event at $w_f=0$. The tail of long jobs decides how badly bursting loses: on Acme, jobs with more than an hour left are \AcmeBurstLongJobsPct\% of the burst jobs but \AcmeBurstLongUsdPct\% of the bill.
\item Findings on prediction. Guided by 90th-percentile runtime estimates, the mix is a median of \LearnedGainMinPct{} to \LearnedGainMaxPct\% cheaper per event than delay on Philly under our default cap curve, and by at most \AcmeLearnedMixGainMaxPct\% on Acme, where exact runtimes would save \AcmeOracleMixGainPctZero\%. Better accuracy did not lower the cost, and reading predictions naively for long-running jobs makes the controller \ContractOverHoldMin{} to \ContractOverHoldMax{} times more expensive.
\end{itemize}
