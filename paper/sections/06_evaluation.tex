\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
All results use the \SetupEventsFeasible{} feasible events; the \SetupEventsInfeasible{} infeasible ones (Section~\ref{sec:method}) are excluded everywhere. Unless stated otherwise, the predictor is the transformer, the lookup rule is hold-beyond-grid, and the cost uses $w_f=0$. Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the cost of each policy and Fig.~\ref{fig:eone} shows all nine cells.

\subsection{Single actions}
In the default cell, delay-only costs \$\DefDelayUsdZero{} per MWh at $w_f=0$ and \$\DefDelayUsdOne{} at $w_f=1$. It is the cheapest of the three single actions.
Cap-only cannot meet the target. Even at the deepest cap level it sheds \DefCapSharePct\% of the target in the default cell, and it is non-compliant in \CapNoncompliantCells{} of the \CellsTotal{} cells.
Burst-only complies but is expensive. In the default cell its per-event cost is a median of \BurstOverDelayDefaultOne{} ($w_f=1$) to \BurstOverDelayDefaultZero{} ($w_f=0$) times that of delay-only; over all \SetupEventsFeasible{} events the medians are \BurstOverDelayAllOne{} ($w_f=1$) and \BurstOverDelayAllZero{} ($w_f=0$) times.
The reason is that the cloud bill covers each burst job's whole remaining runtime, while the grid values only the hours of the event.

\subsection{The mix controller}
With the 90th-percentile cloud bill, the mix is cheaper than delay-only. Its per-event cost ratio to delay-only has a median of \MixPNineOverDelayZero{} at $w_f=0$ and \MixPNineOverDelayOne{} at $w_f=1$. It is cheaper in \MixPNineOverDelayCheaperZero{} and \MixPNineOverDelayCheaperOne{} of the \SetupEventsFeasible{} events. Summed over all events, its cost is \MixPNineOverDelayReductionPctZero\% ($w_f=0$) and \MixPNineOverDelayReductionPctOne\% ($w_f=1$) lower than that of delay-only.
These are different statistics. The ``about 5\%'' gain is the median of per-event ratios; the total-cost reduction is larger. The \$/MWh columns of Table~\ref{tab:main} are medians of the default cell, so they need not agree with the ratio columns.
With the median cloud bill, the mix does not help: its median ratio is \MixOverDelayZero{} at $w_f=0$, and it is cheaper in only \MixOverDelayCheaperZero{} events. With the last-two-jobs predictor the mix costs \MixLastTwoOverDelayZero{} times as much as delay-only at $w_f=0$.

\subsection{Where the gain comes from}
Mix-nocloud runs the same controller with no cloud capacity. For the transformer with the p90 bill, the median ratio of mix to mix-nocloud is \MixOverNocloudTransPNineZeroZero{} at $w_f=0$ and \MixOverNocloudTransPNineZeroOne{} at $w_f=1$. The gain over delay-only therefore comes from capping and from choosing which jobs to suspend, not from bursting: mix-nocloud alone has a median ratio to delay-only of \NocloudOverDelayZero{} ($w_f=0$) and \NocloudOverDelayOne{} ($w_f=1$).
In our runs, bursting helped only with the oracle's exact runtimes. With the oracle, the median ratio of mix to mix-nocloud is \MixOverNocloudOracleZero{} over all events at $w_f=0$, and \MixOverNocloudOracleFourThirtyZero{} and \MixOverNocloudOracleFourTwentyZero{} in the 4\,h cells with 30\% and 20\% cuts. At $w_f=1$ it is \MixOverNocloudOracleOne{} overall and \MixOverNocloudOracleFourThirtyOne{} at 4\,h and 30\%.
The cloud limit of \SetupCloudCapGpus{} GPUs also matters: burst-only complies in \BurstThirtyCompliantEvents{} of the \BurstThirtyEvents{} events with a 30\% cut. In a diagnostic run on \DiagCapThreeKEvents{} such events (last-two-jobs predictor, contract rule) with 3,000 cloud GPUs, burst-only complied in \DiagCapThreeKCompliantEvents{}, at a cloud bill of \$\DiagCapThreeKCloudKusdMin{}k to \$\DiagCapThreeKCloudKusdMax{}k per event.

\subsection{Predictions for long-running jobs}
How a prediction is read beyond the last grid point decides the result. Under the contract rule, the transformer mix costs \ContractOverHoldMin{} to \ContractOverHoldMax{} times as much per event as under the hold-beyond-grid rule, depending on the cloud-bill quantile and $w_f$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:selectors}).
The cause is the running population at an event: \MotRunningBeyondGridJobsPct\% of the running jobs have run longer than 12\,h. Under the contract rule their predicted remaining runtime falls to the 60\,s floor. In the default cell, \ContractBurstFloorMinPct\% to \ContractBurstFloorMaxPct\% of the jobs that the mix bursts are at this floor, against \ContractRunningFloorMinPct\% to \ContractRunningFloorMaxPct\% of all running jobs. Their actual remaining runtime has a median of \ContractBurstActualRemMinH{} to \ContractBurstActualRemMaxH{} hours, so the cloud bills many hours that the controller did not expect.

\subsection{Prediction accuracy and decision cost}
The learned predictors differ little in accuracy; in the default cell they also differ little in decision cost, and the large gaps are to last-two-jobs and to the oracle. On the test split, gradient-boosted trees have the lowest pinball loss (\PredGbdtPinball{}, against \PredLightgbmPinball{} for LightGBM, \PredTransformerPinball{} for the transformer and \PredLastTwoPinball{} for last-two-jobs). The transformer has the highest AUROC for Failed jobs (\PredTransformerAuroc{}, against \PredLightgbmAuroc{}, \PredGbdtAuroc{} and \PredLastTwoAuroc{}).
In the default cell, with the median cloud bill, the median mix cost per event is \$\EthreeTransformerMixUsdEvent{} with the transformer, \$\EthreeGbdtMixUsdEvent{} with gradient-boosted trees, \$\EthreeLightgbmMixUsdEvent{} with LightGBM and \$\EthreeLastTwoMixUsdEvent{} with last-two-jobs. The oracle reaches \$\EthreeOracleMixUsdEvent{}.
The gain of the p90 mix is not specific to the transformer. Over all events, its median ratio to delay-only with the same predictor is \LearnedGbdtMixOverDelayZero{} with gradient-boosted trees and \LearnedLightgbmMixOverDelayZero{} with LightGBM at $w_f=0$ (\LearnedGbdtMixOverDelayOne{} and \LearnedLightgbmMixOverDelayOne{} at $w_f=1$), against \MixPNineOverDelayZero{} and \MixPNineOverDelayOne{} with the transformer. Measured against the transformer's delay-only, the two tree models give \LearnedGbdtMixOverTransDelayZero{} and \LearnedLightgbmMixOverTransDelayZero{} at $w_f=0$. In our runs, the tree models therefore gave a larger gain than the transformer over all events.

\subsection{Robustness}
\emph{Cost weighting.} At $w_f=1$ the ranking of the single actions and the gain of the p90 mix are the same (Table~\ref{tab:main}); the median-bill mix reaches a ratio of \MixOverDelayOne{} there.
\emph{Lookup rule.} The contract rule changes the learned-predictor results (above), not the oracle's.
\emph{Clean jobs.} If only jobs submitted in the test window may be burst, so that every burst decision uses out-of-sample predictions, the median ratio of the p90 mix to delay-only in the default cell is \OosMixPNineOverDelayZero{} ($w_f=0$), as in the main run (\DefMixPNineOverDelayZero{}).
\emph{Jobs without predictions.} If these jobs get fixed uninformed values (30 days of remaining runtime, no failure risk) instead of their true values, the median ratio of the p90 mix to delay-only is \RobustUninfMixOverDelayZero{} ($w_f=0$) and \RobustUninfMixOverDelayOne{} ($w_f=1$), unchanged. All of these jobs are censored and treated as Pass, and they have hundreds of hours left at every event, so both fallbacks lead to the same decisions.
\emph{In-sample jobs.} Jobs submitted before the validation period were in the learned models' training set; at an event start they are a median of \InsampleRunningJobsPct\% of the running jobs and \InsampleRunningGpusPct\% of the running GPUs. If the learned predictors give these jobs the same uninformed values, the median ratio of the p90 mix to delay-only is \InsampleMixOverDelayZero{} ($w_f=0$) and \InsampleMixOverDelayOne{} ($w_f=1$), and the mix is cheaper in \InsampleMixCheaperZero{} and \InsampleMixCheaperOne{} events.
