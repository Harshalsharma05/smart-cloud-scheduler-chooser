\section{Discussion, Limitations and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{When is offloading worth using?} In our runs, bursting lowered the cost only when the controller knew the exact remaining runtime of each job, and mainly in 4-hour events with 20--30\% cuts. It also needs enough cloud capacity for the cut: with \SetupCloudCapGpus{} cloud GPUs, burst-only did not comply in any event with a 30\% cut. A lower cloud price helps only with exact runtimes: at the spot price burst-only still costs \PriceSpotBurstOverDelay{} times as much as delay-only in the default cell.

\emph{Limitations.} We use one trace and one month of test data. The simulator has no virtual-cluster quotas, placement constraints or start-up delay, so its waits differ from the trace (Section~\ref{sec:method}). The GPU pool is constant. The prediction grid ends at 12\,h, while \MotRunningBeyondGridJobsPct\% of the running jobs at an event have run longer. Our default plan ignores $w_f$; at $w_f=1$ a delay-only controller without predictions is then cheaper than the transformer mix and about as cheap as the tree-model mixes, while a plan that uses $w_f$ beats it by \PlanwfGainMinPct{}--\PlanwfGainMaxPct\% (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). The transformer was trained with one seed. Jobs submitted in the training period appear at test events; masking their predictions moved the median ratio of the p90 mix to delay-only by at most \LearnedMaskShiftMax{} for each of the three learned predictors (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). Host power, the cap curve and the migration and restore times are assumptions (Table~\ref{tab:params}). Burst jobs never return on premise. The carbon check uses annual average intensities and event-window energy only. The cloud price comes from a listing site.

\emph{Conclusion.} Delay is the cheapest single way for a GPU cluster to cut power on request; capping alone falls short and bursting alone costs a median of \BurstOverDelayDefaultOne{} to \BurstOverDelayDefaultZero{} times as much as delay per event in the default cell. Guided by 90th-percentile runtime estimates, a controller that combines delay and capping is a median of \LearnedGainMinPct{} to \LearnedGainMaxPct\% cheaper per event than delay alone at $w_f=0$, under our default cap curve, depending on the learned predictor, and offloading adds almost nothing to that gain. Bursting helped only with exact runtimes in long, deep events, and reading predictions naively for long-running jobs made the controller \ContractOverHoldMin{} to \ContractOverHoldMax{} times more expensive.
