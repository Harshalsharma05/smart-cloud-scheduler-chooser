Power grids increasingly ask large consumers to cut load at evening peaks, and GPU clusters are large, fast-growing loads. A cluster can respond by delaying jobs, capping GPU power, or bursting running jobs to rented cloud GPUs. Delay and capping have been studied; offloading as a priced grid action has not. We compare the three in dollars per MWh shed, using trace-driven simulation of the Microsoft Philly cluster with events of 1–4 hours and 10–30% cuts, and build a controller that mixes them and chooses jobs using predicted remaining runtime and failure risk from a small transformer over each user's job history. Delaying jobs is the cheapest single action (roughly $3k–13k per MWh for a 2-hour, 20% cut, depending on how delaying failing jobs is charged). Power capping alone reaches less than half of a 20% cut, and bursting alone costs 30 to 120 times more, because the cloud bill covers a job's whole remaining runtime. A mixed controller that uses the transformer's uncertainty is only a few percent cheaper than delay alone, and reading runtime predictions naively for long-running jobs makes it far worse. We derive guidelines for when offloading is worth using.
