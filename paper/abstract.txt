Grids increasingly ask large consumers, including GPU clusters, to cut evening-peak load. A cluster can delay jobs, cap GPU power, or burst jobs to the cloud. Offloading as a priced action is understudied. We compare the three in dollars per MWh shed, using trace-driven simulation of the Microsoft Philly cluster with events of 1–4 hours and 10–30% cuts, and build a controller that mixes them and chooses jobs using remaining-runtime and failure-risk predictions from learned models (a small transformer over users' job histories, and gradient-boosted trees). Delay is the cheapest single action (roughly $3k–13k per MWh for a 2-hour, 20% cut). In that cell, power capping alone reaches less than half of the cut, and bursting alone costs 30 to 125 times more, because the cloud bill covers a job's whole remaining runtime. Guided by 90th-percentile runtime estimates, the controller beats delay alone by a median of 5–16% per event, depending on the learned predictor, and offloading adds almost nothing to that gain; bursting helps only with near-perfect runtime knowledge, in long, deep events. A naive reading of runtime predictions for long-running jobs makes the controller 10 to 70 times more expensive. We discuss when offloading is worth using.
